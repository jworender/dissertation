# Anticipated Dissertation Committee Questions

This document ranks the 20 committee questions that are most likely to matter for the current dissertation draft. The ranking combines two factors: how likely the question is to be asked, and how much damage a weak answer would do in a defense.

The answers below are intentionally scoped to the manuscript's final claims, not to stronger claims that might have appeared in earlier draft language. The ranking was informed by the review material in `documentation/`, but the evidentiary sources listed under each answer are limited to the dissertation manuscript and supporting references.

## 1. What is the dissertation's actual novelty beyond combining three earlier papers?

**Best answer:** The defended novelty is not "I invented an entirely new optimizer." The novelty is the dissertation-level synthesis and the additional evidence that turns a paper sequence into one coherent, defense-ready framework. The manuscript unifies critical-range rectification, sparse logistic fitting, and anytime rule compression into one longitudinal pipeline, then adds the material that was not present in the prior papers: repeated-resample stability and ablation, expanded sparse baselines, direct interpretable-model baselines, cross-domain transfer plus the Goose Bay protocol audit, empirical boundary-condition stress tests, a strict held-out compression validation policy, an end-to-end workflow for new datasets, and an appendix-level regeneration map. In computer-science terms, the distinctive contribution is representation-first feature redesign, reuse of mature sparse solvers, and policy-controlled interpretable compression in one auditable system. So the dissertation's novelty is best defended as a strong integrated pipeline with scoped theory and explicit deployment governance, not as a claim to replace every adjacent method family.

## 2. Why intervene at the representation level instead of just using elastic net, adaptive lasso, group lasso, or ordered lasso?

**Best answer:** Because penalty redesign and representation redesign attack different failure modes. Elastic net, adaptive lasso, grouped penalties, and ordered penalties all work in coefficient space after the raw correlation geometry has already been handed to the optimizer. This dissertation's claim is that, in threshold-mediated longitudinal settings, the raw representation itself is part of the problem. Rectification changes the feature space before fitting by replacing continuous lagged values with indicators that encode event-relevant threshold semantics. That does two useful things at once: it gives the model a representation closer to the target logic, and it lets the downstream learner stay simple and modular. The synthetic baseline package supports that argument empirically: raw lasso, elastic net, adaptive lasso, group lasso, ordered-prefix selection, and QP+L1 all remain reasonably predictive on the benchmark, but none recover the planted lags exactly, whereas rectified L1 is the only method in that package with the best AUC, the best Youden's J, and nonzero exact-lag recovery. So the dissertation is not anti-penalty engineering; it argues that the intervention point matters.

**Sources:** `Chapters/04_approach.tex` (Why a Representation-First Pipeline; Stage 2), `Chapters/05_rq1.tex` (Expanded correlated-feature baseline package; Table `tab:d002_baselines`), `Chapters/06_rq2.tex` (How this differs from penalty-only fixes), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (Positioning Relative to Prior Work), `references/HZou2005Regularization.md`, `references/HZou2006adaptive.md`, `references/RTibshirani2016ordered.md`, `references/katrutsa2017.md`.

## 3. Is the answer to RQ1 really "yes," or is it too conditional to count as a strong result?

**Best answer:** The correct defense answer is "yes, conditionally," and the dissertation is stronger because it says that explicitly. The manuscript does not claim universal dominance of rectification over raw sparse fitting. It claims that rectification is most useful when the event process is threshold-mediated and lag-structured, and that this claim is supported by both synthetic and real evidence. In the synthetic repeated-resample package, rectification materially improves discrimination, exact-lag fidelity, support stability, and runtime. In HAI, transformed models improve both discrimination and attribution concentration relative to untransformed ones. But the same manuscript also shows boundary cases: UNICEF favors the raw model on raw discrimination, and the generic cross-domain ionosphere probe is negative under standardized defaults. That is not a fatal weakness; it is the dissertation's scope boundary. The committee answer should therefore be that RQ1 is positive in the target regime and intentionally non-universal outside it.

**Sources:** `Chapters/01_introduction.tex` (Executive Map), `Chapters/05_rq1.tex` (Real-world evidence and interpretability tradeoffs; Cross-domain transfer and the Goose Bay protocol audit; Answer to RQ1), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (RQ1 conclusion; Scope Boundaries and Limitations), `references/JOrender2025Efficient.md`.

## 4. Why does the dissertation emphasize attribution stability and lag fidelity rather than only AUC?

**Best answer:** Because the dissertation's problem is not "can I get a good classifier?" It is "can I recover a reliable, interpretable feature-lag structure under multicollinearity?" Zhao and Yu and later dependence reviews make clear that lasso can predict well while still failing at support recovery. That is exactly why Chapter 5 treats AUC and Youden's J as necessary but insufficient. The manuscript reports exact-lag F1, pairwise support Jaccard, support size, and selection-frequency heatmaps alongside discrimination. The repeated-resample ablation shows why that matters: the raw model already has high mean AUC at `0.986`, yet its exact-lag F1 is only `0.048` and pairwise exact-support Jaccard is `0.056`. Rectification raises those to `0.619` and `0.452`, and the compressed rule reaches `0.759` and `0.600`. So the defense answer is that the dissertation evaluates the quantity it actually claims to improve: reliable lag attribution, not merely ranking quality.

**Sources:** `Chapters/01_introduction.tex` (Evaluation Priorities), `Chapters/05_rq1.tex` (RQ1 Evaluation Framework; Repeated-resample stability and stage-wise ablation; Table `tab:d001_stability`), `Chapters/06_rq2.tex` (Why RQ2 matters after RQ1 evidence; IC in computer-science terms), `references/PZhao2006model.md`, `references/freijeirogonzalez2022.md`.

## 5. How should a practitioner decide when rectification is worth trying on a new dataset?

**Best answer:** The dissertation now gives an explicit operating heuristic rather than asking the reader to infer one. Rectification is a strong candidate when event windows repeatedly occupy relatively narrow one-sided or interval ranges at plausible lags, raw sparse fits remain reasonably predictive but their supports drift across folds or nearby tuning choices, the threshold has domain meaning, and a train-only pilot rectification preserves held-out discrimination while improving support concentration, lag localization, or compactness. By contrast, the workflow says to stay raw or move to hybrid features when no stable lag-localized event ranges appear, when performance changes erratically under small threshold adjustments, or when a pure indicator representation causes held-out loss without compensating interpretability gains. The key point is that the pipeline is not a mandate to rectify everything. It is a controlled branch procedure with explicit fallback paths.

**Sources:** `Chapters/01_introduction.tex` (Executive Map), `Chapters/04_approach.tex` (Applying the pipeline to a new dataset; quick-reference checklist; Table `tab:workflow_defaults`), `Chapters/05_rq1.tex` (A practical diagnostic for when rectification is worth trying), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (Scope Boundaries and Limitations).

## 6. How do you defend the mixed real-world and cross-domain results, especially UNICEF and the generic ionosphere probe?

**Best answer:** By treating them as part of the contribution rather than as something to hide. The manuscript's final position is that rectification is strongest in threshold-mediated regimes and weaker when the underlying structure is more additive or when default rectifier settings are mismatched to the domain. UNICEF is the clearest example of the first case: the raw model has stronger raw discrimination, while the transformed model remains sparser and easier to interpret. The standardized cross-domain package makes the same point across multiple datasets: rectification improves some domains strongly, is near-neutral in others, and loses ground in some settings, including the generic ionosphere probe. That mixed panel is scientifically valuable because it converts the thesis from an overgeneralized method claim into a scoped applicability statement. In a defense, the best answer is that the dissertation is stronger because it explicitly reports where the method helps, where it is neutral, and where it should not be oversold.

**Sources:** `Chapters/05_rq1.tex` (Real-world transformed vs. untransformed results; Cross-domain transfer and the Goose Bay protocol audit), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (RQ1 conclusion; Scope Boundaries and Limitations), `references/JOrender2025Efficient.md`, `references/freijeirogonzalez2022.md`.

## 7. Why is the Goose Bay result not a replication failure of the 2022 paper?

**Best answer:** Because the negative generic ionosphere probe and the positive 2022 Goose Bay result are not the same experiment. Chapter 5 makes that distinction explicit. The published 2022 result moved from `J=0.695` to `J=0.854`, a gain of `0.159`, under the original study protocol. The standardized generic transfer probe moved from `J=0.775` to `J=0.716`, a loss of `0.059`, under a deliberately portable default protocol. The robustness audit shows that split matching alone does not recover the published gain; the major movement comes from rectifier semantics and penalty strength. Under the same split with the min/max-style rectifier, the mean gain rises to `0.066`, and with `C=10` it rises to `0.148` and is positive on every tested seed. So the correct defense answer is not "the earlier paper was wrong." It is "Goose Bay is a protocol-sensitivity case showing that portable defaults can obscure a strong rectified solution."

**Sources:** `Chapters/05_rq1.tex` (Cross-domain transfer and the Goose Bay protocol audit; Figures `fig:d002_ionosphere_protocols`, `fig:d002_goose_bay_protocols`, `fig:d002_goose_bay_quantiles`, and `fig:d002_goose_bay_c_sweep`), `references/orender2022.md`.

## 8. What exactly does RQ2 prove, and why is the irrepresentable condition the right lens?

**Best answer:** RQ2 proves a scoped mechanistic result, not a universal theorem about all thresholding rules. In the zero-threshold sign-binarized regime under joint normal assumptions, the dissertation shows a plausible path from rectification to better sparse recovery: the arcsin relation contracts pairwise dependence, inactive-active leakage is reduced, active-block inverse amplification is controlled in the analyzed positive-correlation regime, and the transformed design becomes at least as likely to satisfy an IC-style interference margin. The reason IC is the right lens is that the dissertation is about support recovery and lag attribution under dependence, not just about predictive fit. IC formalizes when inactive features can masquerade as active ones after accounting for active-feature geometry. In lag-expanded data that is exactly the failure mode of interest. So the theorem is a theory of attribution reliability under dependence, not just a theory of classification performance.

**Sources:** `Chapters/06_rq2.tex` (IC in computer-science terms; Why sign binarization can reduce dependence; Main theorem; Practical interpretation), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (RQ2 conclusion), `references/PZhao2006model.md`, `references/JOrender2025Efficient.md`, `references/RAHorn2012Matrix.md`.

## 9. What does RQ2 not prove, and where are the hard boundaries of the theory?

**Best answer:** The theory does not prove universal improvement for arbitrary nonzero thresholds, arbitrary interval rules, every correlation-sign pattern, or every finite-sample high-dimensional dataset. The assumptions are deliberately restrictive: joint normality after standardization, fixed support in the local analysis, large-sample covariance approximations, and meaningful zero-threshold sign binarization. The manuscript is explicit that these are modeling assumptions for tractability, not empirically verified prerequisites for all uses of the pipeline. It is also explicit that negative-correlation behavior near the pole is outside the clean positive-correlation proof path, and that complement features are an engineering repair rather than a hidden corollary of the theorem. A strong defense answer is therefore: the theoretical chapter establishes a credible mechanism and then tells the reader exactly where theorem-level language must stop.

**Sources:** `Chapters/04_approach.tex` (Scope and boundary of the proof), `Chapters/06_rq2.tex` (Tractable Setup and Assumptions; What this theorem does not claim; Negative correlation and the complement-feature repair; Table `tab:rq2_boundary_summary`), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (Scope Boundaries and Limitations), `references/JOrender2025Efficient.md`, `references/freijeirogonzalez2022.md`.

## 10. How do you justify moving from the exact zero-threshold sign theory to the practical critical ranges used in the experiments?

**Best answer:** The dissertation does not pretend that the exact arcsin result automatically generalizes to every practical threshold. Instead, it uses the exact sign case as the theorem anchor and then adds an empirical threshold bridge. The main finding is that the bridge is strongest for one-sided practical rules and weaker for bounded intervals. In the controlled Gaussian pair used in the bridge study, raw correlation `0.599` becomes `0.408` under exact zero-threshold sign binarization and remains directionally contractive at `0.379` for a shifted one-sided threshold `t=0.75`. Interval rules still contract correlation in the reported examples, but their behavior is weaker and more prevalence-sensitive. That is why the dissertation's final language says the cleanest practical continuation of the theory is through one-sided threshold semantics, while interval rules require more caution and more empirical support.

**Sources:** `Chapters/06_rq2.tex` (Threshold bridge: from exact sign theory to practical critical ranges; Table `tab:rq2_boundary_summary`), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (RQ2 conclusion), `references/JOrender2025Efficient.md`.

## 11. Why are direct interpretable baselines like EBM, RuleFit, and greedy rule lists not fatal to the dissertation's claim?

**Best answer:** Because the dissertation does not claim to dominate every transparent model on raw fit. The direct baseline package was added precisely to test that. EBM is the strongest counterexample in the manuscript: on the synthetic Case 1 benchmark it reaches mean AUC `1.000` and mean Youden's J `0.987`, which is slightly better than CUTLASS logic. But the same benchmark shows why that does not collapse the dissertation's argument. EBM keeps all `440` exact-lag additive terms active and takes about `33` seconds, whereas CUTLASS logic reaches mean AUC `0.994`, mean J `0.974`, and only about `6` primitive conditions. RuleFit is faster but materially weaker on exact-lag fidelity, and the greedy rule list is compact but poor on discrimination. So the defended claim is narrower and more defensible: in the threshold-aligned regime, the pipeline gives the best compact attribution-performance tradeoff, not the absolute best raw fit.

**Sources:** `Chapters/05_rq1.tex` (Direct interpretable-model baseline package; Table `tab:d010_interpretable`; Figures `fig:d010_summary`, `fig:d010_selection_frequency`, and `fig:d010_tradeoff`), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (RQ1 conclusion), `references/CRudin2019Stop.md`, `references/EAngelino2018Learning.md`.

## 12. Why not use direct rule-learning or LAD-style methods instead of sparse fitting followed by compression?

**Best answer:** Direct rule-learning methods are important comparators, and the dissertation acknowledges that they can produce strong transparent models. The reason not to make them the primary engine here is scalability and search geometry. LAD-style methods and optimal rule-list methods search directly in combinatorial rule space. That is attractive when the feature space is already modest and structured, but it can become expensive in a lag-expanded longitudinal setting with many thresholded candidates. The dissertation's alternative is to use a convex sparse front end to shrink the search space first, then compress only the active support into a rule artifact. That is why the conclusion describes the work as a bridge architecture between sparse statistical learning and rule-level interpretability. The claim is not that direct rule search is invalid; it is that sparse-front-end filtering plus policy-controlled compression is a better fit for this high-dimensional longitudinal regime.

**Sources:** `Chapters/03_relatedwork.tex` (Interpretable Modeling and Rule-Learning Literature; Table `tab:method_family_positioning`), `Chapters/07_rq3.tex` (Relationship to Adjacent Interpretable Methods), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (Positioning Relative to Prior Work), `references/EAngelino2018Learning.md`, `references/EBoros1997Logical.md`, `references/EBoros2000implementation.md`.

## 13. Isn't logic polishing heuristic, since it orders features by `|beta|` and snaps them to a common magnitude?

**Best answer:** Yes, it is heuristic in the sense that it is not claiming global optimality in rule space. The dissertation's defense is that the heuristic is disciplined, transparent, and embedded in an auditable policy. The sparse model first identifies an active support under a standard convex objective. Magnitude ordering then provides a simple and reproducible way to scan nested prefixes of that support, and vote-threshold optimization turns each prefix into a concrete `m`-of-`k` candidate. The crucial point is that the dissertation does not equate heuristic nomination with deployment. A candidate rule is only adopted if it clears the held-out non-inferiority gates. So the scientific contribution is not "this is the globally optimal rule learner." It is "this gives a controlled complexity-performance frontier and an explicit accept/reject rule for deployment." That is a different, but still defensible, claim.

**Sources:** `Chapters/07_rq3.tex` (From Rectified Sparse Models to Rule Space; Algorithmic Pipeline; Why the Method Is "Anytime"; Limitations and Scope), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (RQ3 conclusion), `references/JOrender2025Anytime.md`.

## 14. How do you define "no material degradation," and why use non-inferiority rather than an ordinary significance test?

**Best answer:** Because failure to reject a difference is not evidence that a simplified model remains acceptable. The dissertation's question is deployment-oriented: after compression, is the rule still fit for purpose under explicit tolerances? That is why Chapter 7 now centers a prespecified one-sided non-inferiority screen. The training frontier nominates the smallest prefix satisfying `J_train(k) >= 0.98 * J_train,base`, and deployment then requires held-out paired-bootstrap lower bounds `Delta AUC > -0.02` and `Delta J > -0.02`. Earlier work in this line used equivalence-style framing, which is why Schuirmann is still part of the citation chain, but the dissertation's final language is more precise: the implemented decision rule is one-sided non-inferiority. That is methodologically appropriate because the acceptance question is asymmetric. The committee answer should be that the manuscript moved from loose "no significant difference" language to an explicit, predeclared loss-margin policy.

**Sources:** `Chapters/01_introduction.tex` (Evaluation Priorities), `Chapters/07_rq3.tex` (Dissertation Strict Policy; Statistical Framing of "No Material Degradation"; Table `tab:rq3_strict_policy`), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (RQ3 conclusion), `references/DJSchuirmann1987comparison.md`, `references/Student1908probable.md`, `references/JOrender2025Anytime.md`.

## 15. If only two of five real-data studies pass the compression gate, is RQ3 really a success?

**Best answer:** Yes, because the defended RQ3 claim is policy-controlled, not "every sparse model should be shortened." The chapter reaches a two-part answer. In controlled synthetic threshold-mediated settings, compression works cleanly and recovers substantially smaller rules without meaningful loss. In real data, the important contribution is the explicit accept/reject policy. Under the strict `2%` rule, HAI `a1` compresses from `39` to `14` terms and HAI `a2` from `25` to `8`, both with held-out lower bounds above the declared margins. HAI `a3`, HAI `a4`, and the ionosphere probe are the cases where the correct answer is "do not compress further." That is not failure; it is exactly what a trustworthy deployment policy should do when the frontier is not flat enough or the uncertainty is too large. The committee answer should therefore be that RQ3 succeeds by making simplification auditable, including auditable rejection.

**Sources:** `Chapters/07_rq3.tex` (Empirical Results; Table `tab:rq3_strict_policy`; Answer to RQ3), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (RQ3 conclusion), `references/JOrender2025Anytime.md`, `references/CRudin2019Stop.md`.

## 16. How do you prevent data leakage in threshold estimation, candidate selection, and compression validation?

**Best answer:** Leakage prevention is built into the workflow and is one of the reasons the dissertation now reads more defensibly than the earlier papers alone. Thresholds are estimated on training data only. Raw-versus-rectified comparisons are made on the same split and operating metric. The candidate compression prefix is selected on training data, but actual deployment requires a held-out paired-bootstrap gate on test data. Chapter 4 also states the conservative defaults explicitly: training-only threshold estimation, cross-validation or stable near-plateau penalty selection, matched splits for raw versus rectified comparisons, and a separate held-out adoption rule for compression. So the committee answer is that the dissertation no longer relies on an informal "we probably did not leak" defense. It presents a train-only representation step and a separate held-out deployment screen as part of the method definition itself.

**Sources:** `Chapters/04_approach.tex` (Applying the pipeline to a new dataset; Table `tab:workflow_defaults`; Worked HAI exemplar), `Chapters/07_rq3.tex` (Dissertation Strict Policy), `references/JOrender2025Efficient.md`, `references/JOrender2025Anytime.md`.

## 17. What are the main failure modes, and what should the analyst do when they appear?

**Best answer:** The manuscript now gives explicit failure modes and explicit fallback branches. The main risk signals are weak threshold structure, erratic sensitivity to small threshold changes, meaningful post-threshold magnitude information that is lost by pure indicators, and compressed candidates that fail the held-out gate. The recommended actions are correspondingly concrete: stay with the raw sparse baseline when threshold structure is weak; use hybrid features when the threshold matters but magnitude still carries signal; stop before compression when the rectified sparse baseline is already good enough or the compressed rule fails validation; and use complement features or out-of-scope language for negative-correlation settings rather than pretending the theorem already covers them. A good defense answer is that the dissertation is strongest where it tells the analyst what not to do, not just what to do.

**Sources:** `Chapters/01_introduction.tex` (Executive Map), `Chapters/04_approach.tex` (Applying the pipeline to a new dataset; troubleshooting rules), `Chapters/06_rq2.tex` (Table `tab:rq2_boundary_summary`; Negative correlation and the complement-feature repair), `Chapters/07_rq3.tex` (Limitations and Scope), `Chapters/89_conclusion.tex` (Scope Boundaries and Limitations).

## 18. How do you defend the dissertation if the committee asks about theory-practice mismatch between sign binarization and real critical ranges?

**Best answer:** The defense should concede the mismatch at the level of exact theorem assumptions and then show that the manuscript already handles it honestly. The theorem is about zero-threshold sign binarization because that is where the arcsin mapping is exact and the IC argument is tractable. The experiments use practical critical ranges because that is the operational problem. The dissertation's answer is not to blur those two objects; it is to connect them in stages. Chapter 4 states that zero-threshold sign binarization is the theorem-scoped special case of the broader empirical rectification step. Chapter 6 then adds boundary checks showing where the proof path is strong, where it is only directionally informative, and where theorem-level language must stop. So the right answer is: yes, there is a theory-practice gap, but the dissertation turns that gap into an explicit bridge and an explicit boundary map rather than hiding it.

**Sources:** `Chapters/01_introduction.tex` (Terminology note), `Chapters/04_approach.tex` (Stage 1; Scope and boundary of the proof), `Chapters/06_rq2.tex` (Threshold bridge; Empirical Implications of RQ2), `references/JOrender2025Efficient.md`.

## 19. Why is this a computer-science dissertation rather than a domain-specific applied statistics project?

**Best answer:** Because the dissertation's main contribution is not a new case study in ICS or radar data; it is a reusable computational pipeline for interpretable longitudinal decision support. The manuscript names its primary technical audience explicitly: ML engineers working with sensor streams, ICS analysts, and clinical-informatics researchers. Its core CS story is architectural and systems-oriented: redesign the representation to change statistical geometry, reuse mature sparse-learning software instead of building a custom solver, and apply a policy-controlled compression layer to produce auditable artifacts for deployment. Chapter 4 makes that story concrete through the engineering summary table, the worked HAI exemplar, the public `cutlass` package, and the regeneration scripts. So the defense answer is that the dissertation contributes an interpretable-ML systems pattern for longitudinal data, with domain datasets used as stress tests rather than as the dissertation's sole intellectual content.

## 20. How reproducible and auditable are the reported results?

**Best answer:** Reproducibility is one of the dissertation's strongest practical defenses. The reusable modeling core is separated into the public `cutlass` package, while the dissertation repository preserves notebook-backed study regeneration for the manuscript-level experiments. Chapter 4 lists the notebook layer, the paired `_generate_*.py` scripts, and the cached outputs under `notebooks/runs_new/`. Appendix C then turns that into a direct manuscript-to-artifact map: for each study package it names the notebook, generator, cached outputs, the figures or tables it supports, and the chapter that consumes them. The minimal rerun pattern is spelled out, including the HAI preprocessing helper when required. So the committee answer is that the work is not merely "code available"; it is auditable at the study-package level, with a direct path from manuscript claim to regeneration artifact.
